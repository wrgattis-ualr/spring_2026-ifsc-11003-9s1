Curious about the Red Planet?

Sending robots to another world is a massive engineering challenge. Here are some of the most common questions about our Martian explorers.

Frequently Asked Questions about Rover Missions

How do the rovers communicate with Earth?

Rovers use high-frequency radio waves to send data to orbiters circling Mars, like the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. These orbiters then relay the data to giant antennas on Earth part of the Deep Space Network (DSN).

Why do they move so slowly?

On average, a rover moves at about 0.1 miles per hour. They move slowly to ensure they don't hit unexpected obstacles and because the signal delay between Earth and Mars (up to 20 minutes) makes real-time "driving" impossible.

How do the newest rovers stay powered?

Unlike older rovers that relied on solar panels, Curiosity and Perseverance are powered by a "nuclear battery" called a Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (MMRTG). This system converts heat from the natural radioactive decay of plutonium into electricity, allowing the rovers to operate day and night, even through massive Martian dust storms.

How do the rovers collect samples without a human laboratory?

Rovers like Perseverance carry a 7-foot-long robotic arm equipped with a rotary-percussive drill. They use this drill to extract core samples about the size of a piece of chalk. These samples are then processed inside the rover's complex "belly" laboratory, where they are hermetically sealed in titanium tubes and cached on the surface for a future mission to return them to Earth.

What happens when a rover "dies"?

When a rover's mission ends—usually because its batteries fail or its solar panels are covered in too much dust—it remains on the surface as a historical monument. In the future, Perseverance's cached rock samples will be retrieved by a separate mission.
